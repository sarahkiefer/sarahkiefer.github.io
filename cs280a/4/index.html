Project 4 — Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF)

CS 280A — Computer Vision & Computational Photography

In this project I implemented an end-to-end Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) pipeline: from calibrating a real camera, to training neural fields that map positions and viewing directions to density and color, all the way to rendering novel views by integrating samples along rays. The write-up is organized into:

  • Part 0 — Data capture & calibration. Build a clean multi-view dataset of a tabletop object using COLMAP.
  • Part 1 — 2D neural field warm-up. Fit a small network that memorizes a single image, exploring positional encoding and capacity.
  • Part 2 — Full NeRF. Train a volumetric neural field on the Lego dataset and then on my own captured scene.

Part 0 — Calibrating My Camera and Capturing a 3D Scan

The first step was to get a solid dataset for NeRF: a calibrated camera and a set of images with accurate poses around a small object. I used an ArUco checkerboard to estimate intrinsics, walked around my object in a smooth loop, and then ran COLMAP for sparse reconstruction and pose estimation. Finally, I exported undistorted, pose-labeled images in the layout expected by the starter code.

What I did

  • Took multiple photos of the calibration board to estimate \(f_x, f_y, c_x, c_y\) and distortion coefficients.
  • Recorded a turntable-style sweep of a tabletop object, keeping distance and step size between views fairly constant.
  • Ran COLMAP’s sparse reconstruction to recover camera poses and a sparse point cloud.
  • Exported undistorted images and camera parameters into the NeRF dataset format for later training.

Camera Frustum Visualizations

COLMAP’s reconstruction can be visualized as camera frustums orbiting the object. Below I show an orbit view and a closer zoom, where you can see the cameras nicely distributed around the scene.

Part 0.1 — Calibrating the Camera

I captured the checkerboard/ArUco target from many angles and used OpenCV’s ArUco utilities to solve for the intrinsics. The resulting calibration file (with focal lengths, principal point, and radial/tangential distortion) is reused by both COLMAP and the NeRF training scripts.

Part 0.2 — Capturing a 3D Object Scan

With the intrinsics fixed, I walked around the object and collected views with roughly even spacing. The goal was to keep the object centered while avoiding big jumps in angle or distance, which helps COLMAP converge and later produces smoother NeRF renderings.

Part 0.3 — Estimating Camera Pose

I passed the calibration and images to COLMAP’s sparse pipeline. After feature matching and bundle adjustment, COLMAP produced per-image camera poses. The frustum visualizations above are derived from these outputs and gave me a sanity check that the reconstruction made sense.

Part 0.4 — Undistorting Images & Building the Dataset

Finally, I undistorted all images and exported them along with poses into the assigned directory structure. This cleaned dataset (intrinsics + extrinsics + RGB frames) is what I feed into the NeRF implementation in Part 2.

Part 1 — Fitting a Neural Field to a 2D Image

Before tackling volume rendering, I started with a 2D neural field that learns a single image. The network takes in normalized 2D coordinates, applies sinusoidal positional encoding, and predicts RGB values. Training samples random pixels, compares predictions to ground truth, and optimizes mean squared error (MSE).

Model & Training Setup

Hyperparameters

  • Pixels per step (batch size): 10,000
  • Training steps: 2,000
  • Optimizer: Adam with learning rate \(1\times10^{-2}\)
  • Hidden width: 256
  • Positional encoding frequencies \(L = 10\)

MLP Architecture

Inputs are PE-encoded 2D coordinates (dim = 42). The MLP processes them through several ReLU layers and outputs normalized RGB: 

MLP(
  fc1: Linear(42 → 256), ReLU
  fc2: Linear(256 → 256), ReLU
  fc3: Linear(256 → 256), ReLU
  fc4: Linear(256 → 3), Sigmoid
)

Reference & Custom Images

I trained on the provided fox image and also on a Golden Gate Bridge photo to see how the network behaves on two very different scenes.

Training Progression

Every few hundred steps, I rendered the current network prediction on the full image grid. Early on, the model roughly captures colors and coarse shapes; later iterations refine edges and textures.

Positional Encoding & Width Sweep

I also swept hidden width and PE frequency to see how they affect sharpness and ability to capture fine structure. Larger widths and higher \(L\) values sharpen tiny features, but also risk overfitting or ringing.

PSNR Curves

PSNR climbs quickly during the first few hundred iterations and then gradually plateaus as the MLP saturates. This matches the visual impression: most structure appears early, with fine-tuning later.

Part 2 — Fitting a Neural Radiance Field from Multi-view Images

With the 2D warm-up working, I implemented a full NeRF for the Lego dataset. The pipeline: (1) load calibrated cameras, (2) shoot rays through each pixel, (3) sample points along rays, (4) predict density and color with a NeRF network, and (5) volume render the result and compare to ground-truth images.

Part 2.1 — Creating Rays from Cameras

Given per-image intrinsics and extrinsics, I created rays by unprojecting pixel centers to camera space, lifting them into 3D, and transforming them into world coordinates. Directions are normalized so every ray is represented as \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{o} + t\mathbf{d}\).

  • A dataset loader normalizes RGB values and stores camera matrices plus intrinsics.
  • pixel_to_camera inverts \(K\) to map UVs onto the image plane at unit depth.
  • transform applies the camera-to-world pose and extracts ray origins and directions.

Part 2.2 — Sampling Points along Rays

For each ray, I sample depth values between near and far bounds, then convert them into 3D positions by \(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{o} + t\mathbf{d}\). An optional perturbation adds jitter to depths for stratified sampling, which reduces aliasing.

  • Randomly sample ~10k rays per iteration to fit into GPU memory.
  • Evenly spaced depths between near and far, with optional noise for stratification.
  • 3D sample points are passed to the NeRF network for density and color.

Part 2.3 — Precomputing Rays

To avoid repeating the same ray math on every step, I precomputed all rays once. A RaysData structure builds grids of pixel centers for each image, converts them to rays, then flattens and caches everything; training just indexes into these tensors.

  • Create a meshgrid of pixel centers with a 0.5 offset for each view.
  • Convert them into origins, directions, and RGB targets, then concatenate over images.
  • Ray sampling reduces to indexing into precomputed arrays.

Part 2.4 — NeRF Network

The NeRF model uses sinusoidal positional encodings for both 3D positions and view directions. Several ReLU layers process the positional encoding with a skip connection, forming a latent feature. One head outputs density (clamped to positive via ReLU), and another head, conditioned on view direction, outputs RGB through a sigmoid.

  • Position encoding maps 3D points to a higher-dimensional space (e.g., 63 channels).
  • View direction encoding adds a smaller set of frequencies (e.g., 24 channels).
  • A skip connection after several layers improves gradient flow for high-frequency details.

Part 2.5 — Volume Rendering

The renderer converts predicted densities into alpha values, accumulates transmittance along the ray, and blends colors using those weights. The implementation avoids in-place operations so autograd can backpropagate through all steps.

  • \(\alpha = 1 - \exp(-\sigma \cdot \Delta t)\) at each sample point.
  • Transmittance is the cumulative product of \((1 - \alpha)\) along the ray.
  • Final color is a weighted sum of per-sample colors with these transmittance-based weights.

Training on Lego

During training, I periodically rendered validation images along with PSNR curves. Early iterations capture coarse geometry, while later ones refine the small Lego details, shadows, and specular highlights.

Takeaway.

Once the pipeline is wired correctly, NeRF transforms the static Lego snapshots into a coherent 3D scene. The orbit render shows consistent geometry and lighting even for cameras never seen during training.

Part 2.6 — Training on My Own Data

After validating the implementation on Lego, I turned the same code on my captured “lafufu” object. I reused the recommended configuration but found that the model plateaued at a relatively low PSNR, so the reconstruction stayed softer and blurrier than the Lego run.

Training Configuration

  • Device selection: prefer MPS, then CUDA, else CPU.
  • num_samples = 64, near = 0.02, far = 0.5 ⇒ step size = (far − near) / num_samples.
  • batch_size = 10,000 rays, learning_rate = 5e-4 (Adam), num_iters = 10,000.
  • chunk_size = 2,048 on MPS (4,096 otherwise) to respect memory limits.

Debugging Attempts

  • Progressive training: starting with fewer rays/samples for quick feedback before scaling up.
  • Tweaked near/far bounds to better fit the captured geometry.
  • Extended iteration counts and tried different learning rate schedules.

Despite these tweaks, PSNR hovered around ~11 dB for this scene, which limited visual sharpness. It’s a good reminder that data quality and coverage matter just as much as model architecture.

Thoughts.

Implementing NeRF end-to-end was a great exercise in combining geometry, sampling, and deep learning. The Lego dataset showcases the full power of the method; my own captures highlighted how sensitive NeRF is to pose quality, coverage, and scene complexity.
