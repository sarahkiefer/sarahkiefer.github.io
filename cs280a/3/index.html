Project 3 — Panoramic Image Stitching

CS 280A — Computer Vision & Computational Photography

This project builds panoramas by (i) collecting overlapping views with pure rotation, (ii) solving for homographies using many point correspondences, (iii) inverse warping with nearest and bilinear interpolation, and (iv) feathered alpha blending into a single mosaic. Important implementation choices I used throughout:

  • Pixel-center convention. I treat pixel centers as integer coordinates; corner math uses ±0.5 where needed.
  • Inverse warping. Avoids holes by sampling the source via \(H^{-1}\) for every output pixel.
  • Bilinear vs Nearest. Bilinear is slower but cleaner (less aliasing). I compare both with timings.
  • Feathered weights. Images contribute most near their centers and taper at the borders for smooth seams.

A.2 — Homography Matrix Computation

For each image pair, I assemble an overdetermined linear system \(A\,\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{b}\) by fixing the \((3,3)\) entry to 1 and stacking two rows per correspondence. Solving with least squares yields the 8 free parameters of \(H\), then I recover the full 3×3 matrix and normalize by \(H_{33}\).

Notes:
  • My code uses all provided correspondences (not just 4) and tolerates large point sets.
  • After solving I report the linear system preview and the estimated homography \(H\).
  • Mean reprojection error (in pixels) is printed per pair to sanity-check click quality.

A.3 — Warp the Images & Rectification

I implemented inverse warping with two interpolation modes: Nearest Neighbor (round to the closest pixel) and Bilinear (weighted sum of the 4 neighbors). The output canvas size is predicted by warping the 4 source corners through \(H\). For rectification, I map a skewed quad in the original to a fronto-parallel rectangle.

Trade-offs. Nearest neighbor is fast but shows aliasing/jaggies; bilinear is slower yet much cleaner on edges and thin details. I also ran into EXIF rotation differences between file viewers — I auto-transpose on load so pixel coordinates match my clicks.

A.4 — Blend the Images into a Mosaic

I build a mosaic by chaining homographies into the first view’s coordinate frame, predicting a global canvas from all warped corner boxes, and inverse warping each image into that canvas. For blending I use simple feathering: an alpha mask that is high near the center of each warped image and falls to zero at its borders (weighted averaging at every pixel). This removes hard seams without fancy pyramids and worked well for these scenes.

Blending notes.
  • I warp a ones image through the same homography to get an occupancy map, then multiply by a feather mask for the final alpha.
  • All images are accumulated in float32 and divided by the weight sum at the end (avoid exposure dips near seams).
  • For wide FOVs you could switch to cylindrical/spherical projection, but these 3–4-view panoramas were fine with planar homographies.
