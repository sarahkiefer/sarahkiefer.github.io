Project 3 — Panoramic Image Stitching

CS 280A — Computer Vision & Computational Photography

This project explores the creation of panoramic image mosaics through projective transformations. We capture multiple photographs with overlapping fields of view and stitch them together to create seamless panoramic images using computer vision techniques. Key components include:

  • Pixel-center convention. I treat pixel centers as integer coordinates; this simplifies the mapping between image pixels and their corresponding points in the scene.
  • Inverse warping. Avoids holes by sampling the source via \(H^{-1}\) for every output pixel.
  • Bilinear vs Nearest Neighbor Interpolation. Bilinear is slower but cleaner (less aliasing). I compare both with timings.
  • Feathered weights for blending. Images contribute most near their centers and taper at the borders for smooth seams.

A.1 — Shoot the Pictures

I shot 3 different settings to derive photographs with projective transformations between them. This means keeping the center of projection stable while just moving the angle of the shot via camera rotation. To make sure I could compute accurate homographies these images needed about 40-70% overlap. I chose to capture a scenic viewpoint, my bookcase, and my backyard shed.

A.2 — Homography Matrix Computation

To derive the homography \(H\), we start from the projective mapping \[ \lambda \begin{pmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = H \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad H = \begin{pmatrix} h_1 & h_2 & h_3 \\ h_4 & h_5 & h_6 \\ h_7 & h_8 & 1 \end{pmatrix}. \] Expanding gives \[ \lambda \begin{pmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} h_1 x + h_2 y + h_3 \\ h_4 x + h_5 y + h_6 \\ h_7 x + h_8 y + 1 \end{pmatrix} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \begin{pmatrix} u \\ v \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{h_7 x + h_8 y + 1} \begin{pmatrix} h_1 x + h_2 y + h_3 \\ h_4 x + h_5 y + h_6 \end{pmatrix}. \] Solving for \(u\) and \(v\) leads to \[ \begin{aligned} u &= \frac{h_1 x + h_2 y + h_3}{h_7 x + h_8 y + 1}, \\ v &= \frac{h_4 x + h_5 y + h_6}{h_7 x + h_8 y + 1}. \end{aligned} \] Multiply both sides by the denominator to linearize: \[ \begin{aligned} (h_7 x + h_8 y + 1)u &= h_1 x + h_2 y + h_3, \\ (h_7 x + h_8 y + 1)v &= h_4 x + h_5 y + h_6. \end{aligned} \] Rearranging: \[ \begin{aligned} h_1 x + h_2 y + h_3 - h_7 x u - h_8 y u &= u, \\ h_4 x + h_5 y + h_6 - h_7 x v - h_8 y v &= v. \end{aligned} \] Each correspondence \((x,y)\leftrightarrow(u,v)\) contributes two equations, giving the linear system \[ \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} x & y & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -xu & -yu \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & x & y & 1 & -xv & -yv \end{pmatrix}}_{A} \begin{pmatrix} h_1 \\ h_2 \\ h_3 \\ h_4 \\ h_5 \\ h_6 \\ h_7 \\ h_8 \end{pmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} u \\ v \end{pmatrix}}_{b}. \]

Remarks:
  • All matched correspondences are used (not just 4) and the system is solved via least squares.
  • \(h_{33}=1\) is fixed and used to normalize the solution.
  • The estimated \(H\) along with the system of equations is shown below for reference.

A.3 — Warp the Images & Rectification

I implemented inverse warping with two interpolation modes: Nearest Neighbor (round to the closest pixel) and Bilinear (weighted sum of the 4 neighbors). The output canvas size is predicted by warping the 4 source corners through \(H\).

Trade-offs.

Nearest neighbor interpolation works by mapping a point in the output image to the nearest pixel in the input image and directly copying its value. Because it only involves rounding coordinates and reading a single pixel, it’s extremely fast and lightweight. However, this simplicity can lead to jagged or pixelated artifacts.

Bilinear interpolation, in contrast, considers the values of the four closest pixels surrounding the target coordinate. It calculates a weighted average based on the distance of the point from each of these neighbors. While this method results in noticeably smoother and more natural-looking images, it comes at a computational cost. Each pixel requires multiple memory accesses and additional floating-point operations to compute the interpolation in both the horizontal and vertical directions.

The performance gap between the two arises from this difference in workload. Nearest neighbor does just one read and minimal math, while bilinear needs four reads and several arithmetic steps per pixel — leading to significantly higher computational overhead. This explains why bilinear interpolation tends to be slower, sometimes by 40–50%, depending on the image size.

A.4 — Blend the Images into a Mosaic

I build a mosaic by chaining homographies into the first view’s coordinate frame, predicting a global canvas from all warped corner boxes, and inverse warping each image into that canvas. For blending I use simple feathering: an alpha mask that is high near the center of each warped image and falls to zero at its borders (weighted averaging at every pixel). This removed hard seams, and though I considered implementing a Laplacian pyramid, it was not necessary based on the results.

Blending notes.
  • I warp a ones image through the same homography to get a map of where the image will be, then multiply by a feather mask for the final alpha.

A.5 — Cylindrical Mapping

Really wide panoramas look better if we first “unwrap” each photo onto a cylinder before stitching. Intuitively, imagine taping the photo onto a soda can and then looking straight at the can—the horizontal perspective becomes more uniform, so the panorama doesn’t stretch or bend weirdly at the edges.

Method (in simple terms)

  1. Choose a focal length \(f\) (in pixels). I start with \(f \approx 0.8\text{–}1.0 \times\) image width and tweak by eye.
  2. Inverse warp to the cylinder. For every pixel on the cylindrical canvas \((u,v)\), map back to the original image \((x,y)\) and sample with bilinear interpolation. This avoids holes.
  3. Stitch on the curved canvases. After pre-warping each image to cylindrical space, I reuse the exact same homography + feathered blend pipeline from A.4 to build the mosaic.
Why it helps. Pure yaw becomes close to a horizontal shift after cylindrical mapping, so wide fields of view stitch with less distortion (especially near the sides).

Formulas (what I implemented)

Let the image center be \((c_x,c_y)\). Normalized coordinates: \(x'=\frac{x-c_x}{f},\; y'=\frac{y-c_y}{f}\).

Forward (plane → cylinder):
\[ \theta=\arctan(x'), \quad h=\frac{y'}{\sqrt{1+x'^2}}, \quad u = f\,\theta + c_x,\;\; v = f\,h + c_y. \] Inverse used for sampling (cylinder → plane):
\[ \theta=\frac{u-c_x}{f},\;\; h=\frac{v-c_y}{f},\;\; x = f \tan\theta + c_x,\;\; y = \frac{f\,h}{\cos\theta} + c_y. \]

I predict the output canvas by forward-warping the source image borders into cylindrical space and taking their min/max. Blending uses the same soft feather mask as in A.4.

Picking \(f\). If you know the horizontal field of view (FOV), \(f \approx \dfrac{W}{2\tan(\mathrm{FOV}/2)}\). Otherwise, \(0.8\text{–}1.0\times W\) is a good starting range; larger \(f\) flattens edges, smaller \(f\) adds more “fisheye.”
