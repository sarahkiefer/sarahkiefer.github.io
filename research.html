Master's Thesis

M.S. Computer Science · UC Berkeley EECS

MilOH: Supporting Teaching Assistants with Retrieval-Augmented Guidance in Office Hours

Advisors
Joshua Hug (advisor) and Narges Norouzi (editor)
Filed
May 15, 2026
Read the PDF Berkeley tech report

Abstract

Office hours are a critical instructional setting in large data science courses, supporting both student learning and teaching assistant development. In an era where students can turn to general-purpose LLMs for fast debugging help or surface-level explanations, the value of office hours increasingly lies in what AI tools alone cannot provide: responsive, human instruction from a teacher who can diagnose confusion, adapt explanations, model expert thinking, and build on the student’s current understanding. At the same time, if fewer routine questions reach staff, TAs have fewer opportunities to observe student difficulties, develop course-specific expertise, and pass down institutional knowledge across semesters.

We introduce MilOH, a retrieval-augmented AI tool that supports TAs during real-time office-hours interactions. MilOH integrates student ticket information with multiple forms of course context, including course materials, prior Q&A resources, and student code retrieved from the course JupyterHub environment. The system generates concise TA-facing guidance that helps staff identify likely issues, relevant concepts, and possible next steps before or during a student interaction. Rather than replacing the TA, MilOH is designed to reduce the time staff spend on mechanical debugging and context gathering so they can focus on the core instructional work of office hours: asking diagnostic questions, giving targeted explanations, modeling problem-solving, and helping students develop deeper understanding.

We evaluate MilOH through a month-long pilot study in Data 100, a large upper-division data science course. Course staff used MilOH in office hours and provided feedback on how the tool shaped their preparation and teaching. Staff found MilOH most useful when it helped them quickly orient to unfamiliar tickets, surface relevant course context, and generate starting points for student conversations. Rather than replacing TA judgment, MilOH helped the TA enter the interaction with a sharper sense of the student’s problem and a clearer path toward helping them.

This work presents MilOH as a human-centered approach to AI-supported teaching infrastructure. By combining course-material retrieval with live student-code context, MilOH helps preserve the distinctive value of office hours as a space for exceptional instruction, while improving the scalability and consistency of support in large data science courses.

Research Experience

  • May 2024 – May 2025

    Competition Networks

    Strang Lab, UC Berkeley · Research Apprentice

    • Building a hypothesis testing framework for the competitive network null model, which has a very specific covariance structure that is shared by many abstract flow networks like financial transactions, information flows in sensor data, air traffic flows, and social interaction networks.
    • Since the null model is so broadly applicable but still has this very specific covariance structure, being able to decide if a network fits that description could speed up computation significantly and offer network insights.
    • Graph theory, subgraph extraction algorithms, test design, Bonferroni correction, uniformly most powerful tests, bootstrapping, and pandas + matplotlib visualizations of test performance across many different metrics.
    • Related work (SIAM)
  • 2024 – 2025

    Reading Group: Fast-Multipole Method vs. Vecchia Transform

    Strang Lab, UC Berkeley

    • Focused on optimization of Gaussian process covariance matrix computation — specifically numerical optimization of O(N²) Gaussian process covariance computation. Led discussions on the Fast-Multipole Method vs. the Vecchia transform.
  • Sept 2022 – Jan 2023

    Gender Bias in Economic Journals

    For Prof. David Romer, UC Berkeley

    • Investigated the biases of the Journal of Economic Literature for Prof. David Romer, who was becoming the editor of that journal; involved data collection and cleaning, EDA, A/B testing, and prediction methods.
    • Probed beyond “is there at least one female author” and required majority female authorship to give real statistics and eliminate bias of the metric. Created 15 different visualizations to aid in understanding the different faces of bias faced by that particular journal; he ended up implementing changes in the areas I laid out.
