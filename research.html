MilOH: Supporting Teaching Assistants with Retrieval-Augmented Guidance in Office Hours

Advisors
Joshua Hug (advisor) and Narges Norouzi (editor)
Department
Electrical Engineering and Computer Sciences, UC Berkeley
Report
UCB/EECS-2026-190
Filed
May 15, 2026
Read the PDF Berkeley tech report

Abstract

Office hours serve as vital instructional settings in large data science courses. In an environment where students access general-purpose language models for debugging and explanations, office hours' true value emerges from human instruction that diagnoses confusion, tailors explanations, demonstrates expert reasoning, and builds on student comprehension.

MilOH functions as a retrieval-augmented tool assisting TAs during office-hour sessions. It combines student ticket data with course materials, Q&A histories, and student code from JupyterHub. The system provides concise guidance helping staff identify potential issues, relevant topics, and instructional directions. Rather than supplanting the TA, MilOH minimizes time spent on routine debugging and information gathering, enabling focus on diagnostic questioning, targeted instruction, problem-solving modeling, and deeper understanding development.

A month-long pilot in Data 100 demonstrated that TAs found MilOH particularly valuable when orienting to unfamiliar tickets and surfacing relevant course materials. The approach represents a human-centered strategy for educational AI support, combining material retrieval with live code context to maintain office hours' instructional distinctiveness while enhancing support scalability and consistency in large data science courses.

Research Experience

Hypothesis Testing for Competitive Networks

Strang Lab, UC Berkeley · Research Apprentice · May 2024 – May 2025

Built and validated a hypothesis-testing framework for the competitive network null model, a structure whose distinctive covariance is shared by many abstract flow networks — financial transactions, sensor information flows, air traffic, and social interaction among them. Because the null model is broadly applicable but tightly specified, being able to decide whether a network fits it can cut computation substantially and yield structural insight.

The work drew on graph theory and subgraph extraction, test design, Bonferroni correction, uniformly most powerful tests, and bootstrapping. I improved code efficiency by roughly 50% by uniting components under a distributed systems framework, and produced the pandas and matplotlib visualizations used to evaluate test performance across metrics. One turn that shaped how I approach problems: reframing the null model's covariance structure as something synthesizable from Kronecker products of subgraphs while preserving index labeling.

Related work: Strang et al., SIAM

Reading Group: Fast Multipole Method vs. Vecchia Transform

Strang Lab, UC Berkeley

Led discussions comparing two approaches to the numerical optimization of O(N²) Gaussian process covariance computation. Reading well outside my domain — the source material leaned heavily theoretical — taught me to extract what matters from a difficult paper and teach it to a room shortly after learning it myself.

Demographic Bias in Economics Publishing

For Prof. David Romer, UC Berkeley · Sept 2022 – Jan 2023

Investigated bias in the Journal of Economic Literature ahead of Prof. Romer taking over as editor. The project spanned data collection and cleaning, exploratory analysis, A/B testing, difference-in-differences, and prediction. Rather than settle for the usual weak metric — whether a paper has at least one female author — I required majority female authorship, which produced statistics that actually held up. I built 15 visualizations covering different faces of the bias; the professor implemented changes in the areas the analysis identified.
